Why avoid KYC: what happens to your ID after you upload it

Updated 18 October 2026 · DoNotKYC

When you pass KYC (know your customer), you give an exchange a scan of your passport or ID card, a selfie and your home address. The exchange keeps those files, and so does the verification company it pays to check them. In the EU, anti-money-laundering law requires them to keep customer records for five years after you close the account.

The files leak

Ledger, 2020. A database leak exposed about one million customer email addresses, and the names, postal addresses and phone numbers of 272,000 buyers. The list was published online. Customers then received phishing emails and letters at home, some with threats. Ledger's statement.

Coinbase, 2025. In May 2025 Coinbase disclosed that criminals had paid overseas support agents to copy customer data: names, addresses, phone numbers, partial bank details and images of government ID. The attackers then demanded a $20 million ransom. Coinbase said fewer than 1% of its monthly users were affected. Coinbase's statement.

A leaked password can be changed. A leaked passport and home address stay valid for years, and a list of people who own crypto, with their addresses, matters to burglars and kidnappers. France saw a series of kidnappings of crypto holders and their relatives in 2025.

KYC follows your coins

Since 30 December 2024, EU rules on transfers of funds (Regulation 2023/1113, the "travel rule") require crypto providers to attach the sender's and recipient's identity to transfers between them. Once one exchange knows who you are, the next one in the chain can learn it too.

Why people skip KYC

  • Fewer copies of your ID: each upload adds one more database that can leak.
  • No document needed: refugees, people without a fixed address and people whose country has no working ID system can still buy and sell.
  • Speed: no waiting days for a manual review.

What skipping KYC does not do

It does not make you anonymous. Bitcoin and most other blockchains are public, and anyone can follow coins from address to address. It does not exempt you from tax or from the law where you live either.

How to limit your exposure

  1. Prefer services with no account: instant swaps, P2P markets and decentralized exchanges.
  2. If you must use an exchange, pick one that asks for ID only above a limit, and stay under it. The directory lists each limit.
  3. Use an email alias for each service, so one leak does not link your accounts.
  4. Send a small amount first and withdraw it to your own wallet before trusting a platform with more.
  5. Keep long-term savings in a wallet you control, not on an exchange.

When KYC makes sense

If you move large amounts between crypto and a bank account, you will almost always need a regulated exchange, and it will ask for ID. In that case, use one exchange instead of five, and keep it separate from the accounts you use for privacy.
